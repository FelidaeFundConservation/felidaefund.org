How America Is Redesigning Its Roads for Wildlife

Whenever a new housing development breaks ground, a familiar question surfaces:, how can growing human populations coexist with wildlife?

  • Author: Charlotte Stevenson
  • Publication Date: July 20, 2026
  • Related Projects: Bay Area Puma Project, Bay Area Bobcat Project, Diablo PumaLink Project, Wild Cat Health Project
  • Focus Species: Lynx rufus  (Bobcat) , Puma concolor  (Mountain Lion)

Fortunately, the conversation is shifting. Rather than debating if large carnivores like pumas belong on the landscape, we are finally asking how we can share space. As urban sprawl fragments natural habitats, isolation has become a primary threat to top predators. But history has taught us that we need them. When West Coast kelp forests collapsed without sea otters, or Yellowstone’s rivers degraded without gray wolves, we learned that apex predators keep ecosystems healthy,  regulating everything from prey populations to disease, soil nutrients, and forest health.

But how do you avoid turning wild habitats into isolated islands surrounded by concrete?

The Super-Predator Factor

As it turns out, pumas are already doing their part to avoid us. Research by Felidae shows that pumas actively alter their behavior to dodge light, noise, and human activity. To a puma, humans are a super predator,  a major risk to be avoided at all costs. Like most wild animals, pumas don’t like being near humans.

Consider Hollywood’s famous mountain lion, P-22. He lived for a decade in Los Angeles’ Griffith Park, surviving entirely on local deer while surrounded by millions of people, proving that pumas can quietly adapt to the edges of our world.

Luckily, some regions are successfully pioneering strategies, even in some of the country's fastest-growing cities. The expansion of housing development into wildlife habitat changes the way pumas use the landscape and forward-thinking planners are taking note. This means prioritizing ecological connectivity, which is the degree to which a landscape facilitates or impedes the movement of animals, plants, and water. Measures include creating wildlife corridors, keeping development open and spacious, and setting up buffers around sensitive habitats like streams.

Local Communities Leading the Way

While some states have passed sweeping conservation laws, like Minnesota’s Buffer Law, which requires counties to enforce riparian vegetation buffers along lakes and streams, the most exciting innovation is happening at the local county and city level. A recent report by the Center for Large Landscape Conservation outlines how towns are successfully weaving wildlife protection into local zoning:

Benefits to city-level co-planning for open space and sustainability include centered growth to reduce travel time, walkable communities, improved air quality, water conservation, and reduced negative wildlife-human interactions. In fact, in 2025, the Center for Large Landscape Conservation found that many smart development strategies appeal across the political spectrum, as ecological connectivity planning also yielded cost savings, raised property values, prevented urban sprawl, and supported climate resilience planning.

Preventing wildlife-vehicle collisions

Even with smart zoning, major highways remain a deadly obstacle for animals with large home ranges. Roads, particularly multilane highways, can isolate animal populations, leading to inbreeding, reduced fitness, and desperate, often fatal attempts to cross these concrete gauntlets.

An estimated 7,000 large wildlife-vehicle collisions occur each year in California alone, costing the state more than $230 million annually, and up to $500 million if you include collisions reported to insurance companies but not reported to the California Highway Patrol. To address these collisions, wildlife crossings have become one of the most effective tools for reconnecting fragmented habitats.

While California already has 100 wildlife crossings, there are still plenty of collision hotspots where new crossings or fencing remain critical. That includes the soon-to-be world’s largest wildlife crossing. the Wallis-Annenberg Wildlife Crossing north of Los Angeles. Currently being built over eight lanes of the busy 101 freeway,  it features two miles of specialized fencing.

Projects like this highlight that overpasses and underpasses are not a one-size-fits-all strategy. Context matters. For example, along the San Francisco Bay Area peninsula, there is almost no suitable puma habitat east of Interstate 280. In cases like this, heavy-duty fencing is actually the smarter choice to prevent dangerous crossings altogether, rather than funnelling animals into urban zones.

The 2025 Connectivity Report details dozens of these successful efforts, both massive and modest, across states like Arizona, Colorado, North Carolina, Virginia, Florida, Utah, Vermont, and Wyoming. Over the next decade, the Wallis-Annenberg crossing and projects like it across the country will do more that just cut down on roadkill; they will allow wildlife to move between habitats without wandering into residential neighborhoods,.

Ultimately, housing development and pumas do not have to be mutually exclusive. These strategies for human-puma coexistence shouldn’t be viewed as sacrifices communities have to make to accommodate wildlife. Instead, neighborhoods across the U.S. are discovering that investing in these solutions brings unexpected lifestyle, economic, and climate resilience benefits, creating a genuine win-win for people and the wild alike.
