How America Is Redesigning Its Roads for Wildlife

Whenever a new housing development breaks ground, a familiar question surfaces:, how can growing human populations coexist with wildlife?

Fortunately, the conversation is shifting. Rather than debating if large carnivores like pumas belong on the landscape, we are finally asking how we can share space. As urban sprawl fragments natural habitats, isolation has become a primary threat to top predators. But history has taught us that we need them. When West Coast kelp forests collapsed without sea otters, or Yellowstone’s rivers degraded without gray wolves, we learned that apex predators keep ecosystems healthy, regulating everything from prey populations to disease, soil nutrients, and forest health.

But how do you avoid turning wild habitats into isolated islands surrounded by concrete?

The Super-Predator Factor

As it turns out, pumas are already doing their part to avoid us. Research by Felidae shows that pumas actively alter their behavior to dodge light, noise, and human activity. To a puma, humans are a super predator, a major risk to be avoided at all costs. Like most wild animals, pumas don’t like being near humans.

Consider Hollywood’s famous mountain lion, P-22. He lived for a decade in Los Angeles’ Griffith Park, surviving entirely on local deer while surrounded by millions of people, proving that pumas can quietly adapt to the edges of our world.

Luckily, some regions are successfully pioneering strategies, even in some of the country's fastest-growing cities. The expansion of housing development into wildlife habitat changes the way pumas use the landscape and forward-thinking planners are taking note. This means prioritizing ecological connectivity, which is the degree to which a landscape facilitates or impedes the movement of animals, plants, and water. Measures include creating wildlife corridors, keeping development open and spacious, and setting up buffers around sensitive habitats like streams.
